# BeforeRSI

**What must be established before recursive self-improvement becomes a scientific claim?**

BeforeRSI is an open research project on self-improvement in AI models, language models, agents, harnesses, learning procedures, and complete AI systems.

The term *recursive self-improvement* currently covers several different phenomena: repeated inference, prompt optimization, memory accumulation, agent search, self-generated training data, weight updates, code modification, and changes to the procedure that produces later changes. These phenomena differ in persistence, transfer, cost, scale dependence, and recursion depth. Treating them as one capability obscures the central questions.

BeforeRSI studies the conditions that must hold before an AI system can be said to improve itself recursively.

## The claim under examination

Let \(S_t\) denote a versioned AI system and let \(I_t\) denote the procedure that proposes, implements, and selects changes. Given experience \(	au_t\) and improvement budget \(B_t\),

\[
S_{t+1}=I_t(S_t,\tau_t;B_t).
\]

A higher score on the tasks used to construct \(S_{t+1}\) establishes little. A transferable improvement requires a gain on an untouched distribution \(Q\), under a fixed evaluation budget \(B_{\mathrm{eval}}\):

\[
\Delta_t(Q)=
U_Q(S_{t+1};B_{\mathrm{eval}})-
U_Q(S_t;B_{\mathrm{eval}})>0.
\]

A recursive claim is stronger. The successor must improve the production of later successors. Let \(G(I,S)\) measure the quality, breadth, or cost-adjusted rate of improvements generated by procedure \(I\) when operating on system \(S\). Evidence for recursion requires a reproducible increase such as

\[
G(I_{t+1},S_{t+1})>G(I_t,S_t),
\]

or a statistically supported positive trend across several generations. One successful update is self-improvement; it does not by itself establish recursive self-improvement.

## Four claim levels

**L0 — Search or adaptation.** The system spends more inference, search, training, or evaluator calls to solve the current task. No persistent successor is required.

**L1 — Persistent self-modification.** The system materially participates in producing a change that remains available to later tasks. The changed object may be weights, code, prompts, memory, tools, data-selection rules, or another component.

**L2 — Transferable self-improvement.** The persistent change improves performance or cost on tasks, distributions, models, or environments that did not participate in selecting the change.

**L3 — Recursive self-improvement.** The improved successor produces later improvements that are better, broader, cheaper, or more reliable than those produced by its predecessor.

The levels describe claims, not project prestige. A careful L1 result can be more informative than an unsupported L3 label.

## Central separations

BeforeRSI keeps several variables separate because each can create apparent progress:

- **Model change and harness change.** Weight updates, context construction, memory, tools, agent topology, search, and control flow can contribute differently and interact nonlinearly.
- **Reusable improvement and additional compute.** Every experiment should compare against giving the original system the same total compute.
- **Transfer and repeated benchmark exposure.** Gains on the improvement distribution must be reported separately from held-out and time-separated gains.
- **Capability gain and evaluator exploitation.** A successor can improve the measured score by exploiting the evaluator without acquiring the intended capability.
- **Small-model success and scale transfer.** A method may disappear, reverse, or become irrelevant as the base model becomes stronger.
- **One-step gain and recursion.** A better task solver need not be a better improver.
- **Average gain and regression.** Improvements in one domain can destroy capabilities in another.

## Research program

The project has three concrete outputs.

1. **A claim-by-claim map of public work.** Each entry records what changed, who or what produced the change, what data was visible, how candidates were selected, what transferred, whether later generations improved, and which artifacts are public.

2. **Experiments that separate competing explanations.** The first experiment compares harness self-improvement against an equal-compute baseline, tests transfer to untouched tasks and another model family, and then measures a second improvement generation.

3. **Reusable evaluation instruments.** Versioned task splits, cost accounting, lineage records, component swaps, regression tests, and machine-readable results make different systems comparable.

The initial public map includes STOP, Gödel Agent, ADAS, AFlow, Darwin Gödel Machine, HGM, HyperAgents, RRSI, Meta-Harness, HSI, OpenRSI, MASS, SIA, SEAL, RSI-Anything, Dream-RSI, and RSIGym. Inclusion means that the work is relevant and publicly inspectable. It does not mean that BeforeRSI accepts every RSI claim made by its authors.

## Minimum evidence for an RSI claim

A serious experiment should expose:

- the exact parent and successor systems;
- the objects permitted to change;
- the model, harness, tools, evaluator, data, seeds, and software versions;
- the tasks visible during improvement;
- untouched evaluation tasks or distributions;
- training, inference, wall-clock, accelerator, token, and API budgets;
- accepted and rejected candidates;
- task-level gains and regressions;
- a same-compute baseline using the parent system;
- at least one later generation when recursion is claimed.

Results are tagged `reported`, `reproduced`, `replicated`, `contradicted`, or `open`. `Reported` means that the original source states the result. It does not mean that BeforeRSI has reproduced it.
